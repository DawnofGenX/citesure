Climate Change is one of the defining issues of our time and we are at a pivotal moment. From shifting weather patterns that threaten food production, to rising sea levels that increase the risk of catastrophic flooding, the impacts of climate change are global in scope and unprecedented in scale. Taking decisive action today will make adapting to these impacts in the future more effective and less costly. What is Climate Change? Climate change refers to long-term shifts in temperatures and weather patterns. Although some climate variations occur naturally, human activities have been the main driver of climate change since the 1800s, mainly through the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and gas. These activities release greenhouse gas emissions into the atmosphere, trapping heat from the sun and causing the Earth’s temperature to rise. Other major contributors to climate change, closely linked to the burning of fossil fuels for energy, include transportation, industrial production, the energy used in buildings, food production systems, and deforestation. Together with growing levels of overconsumption and waste, these activities significantly increase greenhouse gas emissions and accelerate global warming. The devastating effects Greenhouse gases, including carbon dioxide and methane, act like a blanket around the Earth. They allow sunlight to enter the atmosphere but prevent some of the heat from escaping back into space. Human activities have greatly increased the concentration of these gases, leading to global warming and disruptions in climate systems around the world. These emissions are already affecting every region of the world. Some of the major impacts include: - More frequent and intense heatwaves - Stronger storms and heavier rainfall: as temperatures rise, more moisture evaporates, exacerbating extreme rainfall and flooding. - Longer droughts - More intense and more frequent wildfires - Melting glaciers and ice sheets - Rising sea levels - Damage to ecosystems and wildlife - Risks to food, water, and human health Vulnerable communities and developing countries are often the most affected, despite contributing the least to global emissions. Approximately 3.3 to 3.6 billion people -43% of the global population- live in contexts that are highly vulnerable to climate change. The Paris Agreement, a UN landmark in the fight against climate change The roadmap to fight climate change worldwide started with the Kyoto Protocol (1997), the first major international treaty aimed at reducing greenhouse gas emissions. But its effectiveness was limited. Almost two decades later, a new landmark UN agreement emerged: The Paris Agreement. Adopted in 2015 by nearly every country, it aims to keep global warming well below 2°C while pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C. This agreement is considered a landmark in international climate cooperation because, for the first time, all countries committed to acting against climate change. The Agreement includes commitments from all countries called Nationally Determined Contributions (NDC) which outline how they will reduce emissions and work together to adapt to the impacts of climate change and calls on countries to strengthen their commitments over time. Why the 1.5°C goal matters, according to science If humanity wants to avoid even more catastrophic consequences, the 1.5°C limit is critical. The 1.5°C goal refers to limiting Earth’s global warming to no more than 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels (1850-1900). This is considered the earliest period for which high-quality observations of surface temperatures over the land and ocean are available. Science proves that every fraction of a degree matters. Beyond 1.5°C, the risks of extreme heatwaves, droughts, floods, wildfires, rising sea levels, food insecurity, and biodiversity loss become much greater. A single month or year above 1.5°C does not mean the climate goal has officially failed. Scientists measure the limit over decades because temperatures naturally fluctuate from year to year. However, repeated breaches are an important warning sign that the Earth is getting dangerously close to exceeding the long-term threshold. In simple terms, it is like having a fever for a day versus having one for many weeks: one hot day is not the full problem, but it can be a sign that something serious is developing. The latest climate science shows that the planet is heading for a temporary overshoot above 1.5 degrees Celsius. Our responsibility is to make that breach as small, as short, and as safe as possible – through a just, orderly and equitable transition away from fossil fuels to clean, renewable energy. The Earth is now about 1.44°C warmer than it was in the pre-industrial era (1850-1900). The world is currently not on track to meet the 1.5°C target. According to recent UN reports, global temperatures are likely to exceed the 1.5°C threshold in the next decade unless greenhouse gas emissions decline much faster. The consequences are already visible in the numbers: - 2015-2025 have been the hottest 11 years on record. - Over the past nine years, each year has set a new record for ocean heat content. - Glaciers are projected to vanish in one-third of the current sites by 2050. - Forecasts estimate that by 2050 droughts may affect over three-quarters of the world’s population. - Between 2030 and 2050, climate change is expected to cause approximately 250,000 additional deaths per year from malnutrition, malaria, diarrhea and heat stress, according to the World Health Organization. And by 2030, the direct health costs caused by climate change are estimated to be between $2 billion and 4 billion a year. It’s been more than a decade since the Paris Agreement was signed and scientists warn that current climate commitments made by the countries are still insufficient to prevent dangerous levels of warming. There is still hope, but faster action is needed Although the world is still far from achieving the 1.5°C goal, progress shows that meaningful change is possible with stronger global action. When the Paris Agreement was adopted in 2015, experts calculated the world was on pace to warm by 3°C to 3.5°C based on countries’ mitigation pledges — a potentially catastrophic path. Thanks to the commitments it inspired, that projection has fallen by about a full degree, showing that global climate action can make a real difference. Experts emphasize that this should be the time to reduce future risks if countries accelerate climate action, transition to renewable energy, protect ecosystems, and strengthen international cooperation. Addressing climate change requires major reductions in greenhouse gas emissions. Governments, businesses, and individuals can contribute by: - Transitioning to renewable energy sources - Improving energy efficiency - Protecting forests and natural ecosystems - Reducing pollution and waste - Investing in sustainable technologies These measures can also create opportunities for cleaner air, better public health, green jobs, and more sustainable economic growth. The UN and climate change The United Nations led the creation of several important organizations and agencies to address climate change globally. Follow all of them to stay updated about the progress on climate change. Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) The IPCC, the scientific body behind climate research, was created by the United Nations Environment Programme (UNEP) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) in 1988. Its role is to assess scientific research on climate change and provide governments with reliable scientific information to guide climate policies. IPCC reports are considered the world’s leading source of climate science. Learn more about the latest comprehensive scientific assessment report United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), adopted in 1992, provides the main international framework for negotiating global climate action. It established the foundation for later agreements such as the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement. Countries that are part of the UNFCCC meet every year at the Conference of the Parties (COP) to discuss progress and negotiate climate policies. Climate change is one of the greatest challenges humanity has ever faced, but it is also an opportunity to rethink how we produce energy, protect nature, and cooperate globally. Scientists warn that the next decade will be decisive. The choices made today by governments, businesses, and individuals will shape the future of the planet for generations to come. Resources
